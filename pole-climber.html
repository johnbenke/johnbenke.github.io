Autonomous Pole-Climbing Robot

Special Topics in Robotics · Team of four · August 2026 – present

An autonomous robot that drives across campus to a light pole, detaches from its driving base, climbs the pole using grippers and a flipping motion, takes a photo from the top, and sends it back to the user.

It is really two autonomous systems in one mission: a drive base that finds the pole, and a climbing module that detaches and goes up it.

My role

  • Mechanical design and CAD lead.
  • Mechanical engineer on the drive base — frame and drivetrain.
  • Electrical integration and soldering.
  • Team and project management, including the bill of materials and the Gantt chart.

How it works

  • The drive base is built from two bicycles, with one powered wheel and handlebars steered by a servo motor, running on its own battery and controlled by an Arduino on a compact rechargeable pack.
  • The climbing module uses servos, grippers and a flipping motion to work its way up the pole.
  • A control board processes what the robot sees and sequences each step of the mission, so it moves itself from one stage to the next without an operator.

Engineering highlights

  • Iterated the gripper through four laser-cut and 3D-printed prototypes against a measured octagonal pole that tapers from 14.5 inches to 8.25 inches — the gripper has to hold at both ends of that range, not just one.
  • Replaced a squeezing claw with a wraparound hook, which cut the servo torque required. Changing the geometry was cheaper than specifying a bigger servo.
  • Split the drive base from the climbing module specifically to cut climbing mass, and set a 4 kg weight budget working backward from servo capacity — the servos you can afford decide what the robot is allowed to weigh.
  • Selected the DC drive, servo and stepper motors.
  • Integrated servos, sensors, a battery pack and the control board, and soldered the wiring.

What I am taking from it

Designing something that has to lift itself is a different problem from designing something that sits on the ground. Every gram added has to be carried by grippers and servos already chosen, so the mechanical, electrical and control decisions keep feeding back into one another — you cannot finish one before starting the next. The weight budget is what made that tractable: once it existed, it settled arguments instead of starting them.
