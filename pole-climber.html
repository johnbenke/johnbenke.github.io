Autonomous Light Pole Climber

Systems & Robotics · Team of four · Senior year · In progress

A robot that climbs a light pole on campus by itself, takes a photograph from the top, and sends it back to the user. It drives to the pole on a wheeled base, separates from that base, and climbs — gripping the pole and working its way up with a flipping motion rather than a continuous crawl.

The whole sequence runs autonomously, so the robot has to know where it is in the task and move itself to the next step without anyone driving it.

  • 3stages: drive, detach, climb
  • 4person team
  • Autonomousno operator input

The sequence

  • Drive to the pole on a wheeled base.
  • Detach the climbing unit from that base.
  • Climb the pole using grippers and a flipping motion — one set of grippers holds while the body flips past it to take the next bite up the pole.
  • Take the photograph and send it back to the user.

What it took to build

  • A lot of servos — the gripping and flipping motion needs coordinated actuation at several joints, and each one has to hold position under the robot's own weight.
  • A battery pack sized for the climb.
  • Sensors so the robot can tell where it is in the sequence.
  • A control board sequencing the steps we want it to carry out, in order, on its own.

Where the difficulty is

This one lives across three disciplines at once. The mechanical side is getting the grip and the flip to actually lift the robot; the electrical side is powering and wiring that many servos; and the systems side is sequencing it all so the robot moves through the task reliably rather than once in five tries. It has taken a lot of prototyping and a lot of back-and-forth — build it, watch where it fails, change it, run it again.

What I am taking from it

Designing something that has to carry itself vertically is a different problem from designing something that sits on the ground. Every gram you add has to be lifted by the grippers you already chose, so the mechanical, electrical and control decisions keep feeding back into each other — you cannot finish one before starting the next.
