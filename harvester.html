Remote-Controlled Sorghum Biomass Harvester

MEMS 4110 Mechanical Engineering Design · Washington University in St. Louis · Team of four · Customer: Dr. Cody Bagnall, Donald Danforth Plant Science Center · Fall 2026 – present

Commercial biomass harvesters cut four to eight rows at a time. That is far too big for the Danforth Center's research plots, where sorghum is grown in single test rows spaced 30 inches apart. The researchers needed something small enough to drive wirelessly down one row — cutting stalks, grinding them down, and storing the biomass on board.

The stalks are 1–4 inches thick and 12–15 feet tall, and the field itself is mud, holes and weeds. Those two facts set most of the design.

My role

  • Mechanical lead for the frame and the drivetrain.
  • I built the drive-wheel engineering model. It sizes the motors from rolling resistance, acceleration, wheel torque, gear ratio, drivetrain efficiency and a safety factor — and it checks traction, so the front drive wheels do not simply spin in a muddy field.
  • Teammates own the cutting-blade and grinding-roller models and the electronics.
  • I maintain the design document end to end and the budget tracker, and report weekly progress and next-week goals to the project supervisor.

The process

  • Interviewed the customer and turned what he told us into 15 requirements — among them 20–25 ft of wireless range, an 8–12 inch cutting height, room for 40–60 stalks, and the ability to handle rough ground.
  • Researched commercial harvesters, relevant patents, and the agricultural machinery safety standards: ISO 4254-7 and ANSI/ASAE S318.19.
  • Built a cardboard mockup and sketched four concepts.
  • Ranked them with an Analytic Hierarchy Process feeding a weighted scoring matrix, so the choice came out of the customer's own priorities rather than ours.
  • The mockup also settled wheels over tracks, on cost and reliability.

The design that won

  • A funnel: guide arms steer each stalk into the blade, and the cut stalk slides down a ramp into grinding rollers that feed a storage bin.
  • Dual-motor tank steering, so the machine turns within its own footprint — which matters more than speed when the rows are 30 inches apart.
  • Three independently reversible motors on plug-and-play ESCs, a 12 V AGM battery and an RC receiver.
  • The frame is built from scratch to carry the machine's weight over field terrain and stay stable with all of the rotating mass on board.

Constraints

  • A $600 total budget, which rules out most of the obvious answers and is the reason the electronics are plug-and-play rather than custom.
  • Safety: shielded blades, and remote operation from a safe distance. On a machine with exposed blades that nobody is standing next to, guarding is a design input rather than an afterthought.

Status

Design and modeling are complete; the prototype build and field testing are still ahead, so there are no test results to report yet.

What I am taking from it

This is cross-disciplinary systems work with a real customer, hard physical constraints and a model-driven design — the mechanical, electrical and budget decisions all constrain each other, and the engineering model is what makes that tractable instead of a matter of opinion. The hard part was never the idea. It is holding the process together: keeping documentation current rather than writing it up at the end, justifying spend as it happens, and standing behind a schedule every week. The cardboard mockup taught me the most in the least time — an afternoon of it answered a question that two weeks of argument had not.
