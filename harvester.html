Remote-Controlled Biomass Harvester

MEMS 4110 Senior Capstone · Sponsored by the Danforth Plant Science Center Field Research Site · Team of four · August 2026 – present

A fully remote-controlled harvester that cuts sorghum stalks in the field, grinds them on board, and stores the ground material until the machine is driven back to the operator and emptied. The output goes to bioenergy or to feed for animals on the farm.

The project is sponsored by the Danforth Plant Science Center Field Research Site, so the requirements came from a real operator working real rows rather than from a brief.

My role

  • Mechanical engineer on the team, responsible for the frame and the drivetrain.
  • I own the drive wheels engineering model; a separate model covers the grinding rollers.
  • I maintain the design document end to end and the budget tracker for all spending, and report weekly progress and next-week goals to the project supervisor.

How it works

  • Dual-motor tank steering, for tight maneuvering between crop rows. Driving the two motors independently lets the machine turn within its own footprint, which matters far more than speed when the rows are 30 inches apart.
  • A cutter at the front takes the stalk, a feed section holds and controls it as it moves inward, a grinder in the middle reduces it, and a storage container collects the ground material.
  • Hardware: two drive motors, two blade motors powering two cutting blades, motor controllers, a remote control and receiver, and a battery.
  • The frame is built entirely from scratch — it has to carry the vehicle's weight over field terrain and stay stable with all of the rotating mass on board.

Design process

  • Interviewed the director of the Danforth Field Research Site and translated that interview into 15 customer needs and a set of target specifications.
  • Key specifications: 30-inch row spacing, stalks 1–4 inches wide, an 8–12 inch cut height, and 20–25 ft of wireless range.
  • Benchmarked three existing harvesters and two patents before committing to an approach.
  • Applied ISO 4254-7 and ANSI/ASAE S318.19 agricultural machinery safety standards to blade guarding and to remote operation — on a machine with two exposed blades that nobody is standing next to, guarding is a design input, not an afterthought.
  • Generated four concepts through functional decomposition and a morphological chart.
  • Built a scale mockup, which settled two decisions: wheels over tracks, on cost and reliability, and how to control the way stalks feed into the grinder.

What I am taking from it

This is the closest thing yet to how design works in industry. The hard part is not the idea — we know what we are building. It is holding the whole process together: keeping the documentation current rather than writing it up at the end, justifying spend as it happens, and standing behind a schedule in front of a supervisor every week. The scale mockup taught me the most in the least time; an afternoon of cardboard answered a question two weeks of argument had not.
