Remote-Control Biomass Harvester

Senior Capstone — Mechanical Engineering Design Project · Team of four · 2026–27 · In progress

A remote-operated machine that harvests a sorghum stalk, grinds it down, and carries the result back to the operator to be emptied. The output is biomass — either feedstock for bioenergy or feed for animals on the farm — so the machine has to do the whole job in one pass: cut the stalk, keep hold of it, grind it, and store it.

The operator never has to walk out to it. It is driven entirely by remote control at a distance, and when the hopper is full it is driven back in to be emptied.

  • Zeroturn radius
  • 4motors
  • 2cutting blades
  • 4person team

How it works

  • Dual-motor tank steering — the two drive motors are driven independently, so counter-rotating them turns the machine within its own footprint. In a crop row, being able to turn without needing space to swing matters more than top speed.
  • A cutter at the front takes the stalk.
  • A holding stage between the cutter and the grinder keeps the stalk under control through the hand-off, so it feeds rather than jams.
  • A grinder in the middle reduces the stalk.
  • A storage container at the back collects the ground material until the machine is driven back and emptied.

What it is built from

  • Two drive motors and two further motors dedicated to cutting.
  • Two cutting blades.
  • Remote control and receiver, motor controllers, and a battery.

How the project runs

  • Full design documentation maintained across the whole project, not written up at the end.
  • A spend sheet tracking the budget as we go.
  • A weekly report to a supervisor covering what we achieved that week and what we intend to achieve the next — which means the plan has to be real enough to be held to.
  • Four-person team.

What I am taking from it

This is the closest thing yet to how design actually works in industry. We know what we are building, so the difficulty is not the idea — it is the prototyping and the design process around it: making the sub-systems hand off to each other cleanly, justifying spend, and standing behind a schedule in front of someone every week. Reporting progress out loud changes how you plan.
