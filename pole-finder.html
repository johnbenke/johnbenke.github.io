Autonomous Pole-Finding Robot

ESE 3050 · Washington University in St. Louis · Team of four · Fall 2026 · Mechanical design, embedded C++, computer vision

Our team is building a two-part system: a robot that locates a pole and delivers a climbing module to it, and a climbing module that goes up. I am responsible for the first half — the robot that finds the pole and gets the climber there. My teammates are designing the climber itself.

Mine is a low-cost autonomous robot built from two bicycles bolted together. With no human input it searches its surroundings, spots a target marked with orange tape, drives up to it, and stops.

  • 3control states
  • 160×120vision frames
  • 2boards, one serial line
  • 2bicycles

Frame and drivetrain — my main role

  • Joined two bike frames with 2×4s to make the chassis.
  • Designed a friction drive: a motor roller presses directly against the tire, which avoided dealing with chains entirely. One wheel is powered.
  • A single servo steers both sets of handlebars together through a linkage.

Asymmetric steering

The robot only ever searches by circling left. So I gave the linkage a lot of left travel and very little right. That trade bought more search turn without losing fine control on the approach — the two phases need opposite things from the same servo, and the geometry can favour one without a second actuator.

Electronics

  • An ESP32-CAM handles vision and an Arduino Uno clone handles drive control, connected by a single serial line.
  • I chose two boards over one because the ESP32-CAM has few usable pins, and because splitting the work made each board easier to debug and to swap when something failed.
  • Power runs from the battery through a fused kill switch, then to separate adjustable buck converters for the logic and the servo, and to a BTS7960 motor driver.

Control software

  • I took over the code and the camera mount late in the build.
  • The ESP32-CAM thresholds 160×120 frames by colour and sends the target's position and pixel count to the Arduino.
  • The Arduino runs a three-state machine — search: drive in a circle until the target is spotted; track: steer toward it in proportion to how far off centre it sits; arrive: stop once the target fills enough of the frame.
  • Safety features: a power-on delay, a ramped motor start so the roller does not slip against the tire, and a failsafe that cuts the motor if the camera stops sending data.

The climbing module

Designed by my teammates. Once my robot has delivered it to the pole, it detaches and climbs using grippers and a flipping motion.

Status

Build and integration are underway; trial results are not in yet, so there are no success rates or approach distances to report.

What I learned

This was an exercise in integrating mechanical and electrical systems on a cheap, flexible frame — and "flexible" is the problem as much as the feature. Vibration affected the camera, power noise reset the boards, and the decision that fixed the most was mounting the camera to the frame rather than to the steering, so the control code was not chasing its own steering inputs. The mechanical choice solved a software problem.
